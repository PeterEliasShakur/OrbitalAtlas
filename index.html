    Orbital primer

    What is an artificial satellite?

    A human-made object placed in orbit: it moves fast enough sideways that, as it falls toward Earth, the surface curves away beneath it. Satellites relay communications, provide navigation signals, observe weather and land, and carry science instruments and crews.

    LEO, MEO and GEO

    Low Earth orbit (up to ~2 000 km) hosts the ISS, Hubble, Earth-imaging satellites and mega-constellations like Starlink; one revolution takes ~90–130 min. Medium Earth orbit (2 000–35 586 km) is home to navigation systems — GPS orbits at ~20 200 km with a 12-hour period. Geosynchronous orbit (~35 786 km) has a period of one sidereal day; over the equator (geostationary) a satellite appears fixed in the sky. Classes in this app are approximate and derived from the orbital elements.

    Inclination

    The tilt of the orbital plane relative to the equator. 0° is an equatorial orbit; 90° passes over the poles; above 90° the satellite moves westward (retrograde). The ISS's ~51.6° inclination is why its ground track never goes beyond ~51.6° N/S. Sun-synchronous Earth-observation satellites use ~97–99°.

    Why do satellites travel at different speeds?

    Gravity weakens with distance, so the speed needed for a circular orbit drops with altitude: v = √(μ / r). The ISS moves at ~7.7 km/s, GPS satellites at ~3.9 km/s and geosynchronous satellites at ~3.1 km/s. In elliptical orbits, speed is highest at perigee and lowest at apogee (Kepler's second law).

    TLE / OMM orbital elements

    A Two-Line Element set (TLE) or its modern equivalent, the Orbit Mean-Elements Message (OMM), describes an orbit at a reference time — the epoch — with mean elements: mean motion, eccentricity, inclination, right ascension of the ascending node, argument of perigee, mean anomaly and the B* drag term. They are not coordinates: positions must be computed from them. This app uses CelesTrak's OMM JSON, which also supports catalog numbers beyond the five digits of classic TLEs.

    SGP4 propagation

    SGP4/SDP4 is the analytic model the elements are fitted with. Given an element set and a time, it predicts position and velocity, accounting for Earth's oblateness, atmospheric drag and (for deep-space orbits) lunar and solar effects. Accuracy is typically around a kilometre at epoch, degrading by roughly 1–3 km per day — so fresh elements matter. Your browser runs SGP4 (via satellite.js) for every displayed satellite, many times per second.

    Telemetry vs. predicted position

    Telemetry is data measured and transmitted by the spacecraft or tracking stations. A predicted position is calculated from orbital elements. Every satellite position in this app is a calculated prediction; the ISS additionally shows the position reported by the WhereTheISS.at API, which is an independent, provider-computed value — not raw spacecraft telemetry. Comparing the two is a good sanity check.

    Reading the visualisation

    The orbital path is one full revolution of propagated positions, drawn in the Earth-fixed frame at the current instant — the shape of the orbit in space. The ground track is the path of the point directly beneath the satellite, which shifts westward each orbit because Earth rotates underneath. Dashed segments are the past, solid segments the future. Rendered altitude may be compressed for legibility; real altitudes are always shown in the details panel.
